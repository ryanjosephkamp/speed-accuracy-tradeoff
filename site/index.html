Deciding when time gets expensive

Every ball is a driverless car that must swerve left or right around something on the road ahead of it. Choose the wrong side, or take too long to decide, and it could be disastrous. A bound is the level the running total of evidence has to reach before the car decides. The best bounds start moving before the cost of time changes.

Ryan Kamp · November 17, 2026

Working draft. This study is still in development. Paper in preparation; draft available upon request. What’s here may change.

Your turn

Now you’re the car. A stalled truck blocks one side of the road ahead, hidden in fog, and its hazard lights glint through: each glint shows on the truck’s side 55% of the time, and as a reflection on the other side for the other 45%. The cost of waiting runs along the bottom. There are three road scenarios. On one road, the cost jumps 16-fold at a dashed line. On another, the truck is close. And on the third, the cost stays steady with the truck far off in the distance. You see which road you’ve been given before you start. Swerve left or right whenever you’re ready. Then the fog lifts, and the three rules drive the same road with the same glints, so you see where each would have swerved. Nine rounds, against all three.

Why the bounds move first

Wait longer and you’re more likely to be right. Wait too long and the time costs more than the accuracy is worth. That trade shapes a lot of decisions made under a clock: a scanner deciding whether it has read a code yet, a driverless car deciding which way to swerve before it reaches something in the road, an animal deciding whether a rustle is a predator.

Often the price of time changes while you decide. When the change is known in advance, the best strategy doesn’t wait for it: it starts settling for less certainty ahead of it, because a decision still going when the price rises will pay it. That the best strategy does this was known (Barendregt and colleagues, 2022, found it numerically); to my knowledge, the paper is the first to work out how far ahead it moves, and the machine above shows it. In a worked example in the paper, the average observer in a classic moving-dot study (Palmer, Huk, and Shadlen 2005), at 6.4% coherence, would move its bounds about 217 ms ahead of a slow, predictable change in the cost, if its bounds are the best ones for a steady cost; that’s the first-order lead.

My 2025 capstone used a rule that sets the bounds from the cost at each moment, so it reacts only when the cost changes. Switch the bounds to the 2025 rule to see it. The rest of this page builds the picture up from one decision, then measures my 2025 rule and a better one.

What it could be good for

The short answer: its clearest value is a prediction about brains that anyone with the right recordings can test. As a rule for machines, the gain over my 2025 rule is real but usually small, and nobody has tried it outside the model yet. Here is where looking ahead matters, in eight working examples and seven solved cases, four of them the paper’s own.

They are grouped by what happens to the cost of time, because that decides how the best bounds move. Each says how well the model fits it: close means the model was made for it, fair that its main assumptions hold, loose that it is an illustration only.

A prediction about brains

The most promising use. If people and animals set their urgency close to the best bounds for a cost they have learned, their urgency should start rising before a known change in the cost of time, not when it arrives. For a slow change, the lead is about 217 ms for the average observer in the moving-dot study at 6.4% coherence; for a cost that swings once a second, about 103 ms; and for faster swings, close to an eighth of a cycle. A rule that reacts to the present cost, like my 2025 one, predicts no lead at all. Choices and response times differ too, but by amounts that change in size and sign from schedule to schedule, so a recorded urgency signal (Hanks, Kiani, and Shadlen 2014) is the more direct test. To my knowledge, nobody has run it, and whether brains use collapsing bounds at all is still debated (Hawkins and colleagues 2015). That the best bounds anticipate a known change was already known (Barendregt and colleagues 2022); the paper adds, to my knowledge for the first time, how far ahead, in closed form.

Machines that decide on a clock

Plausible, and small. A sensor deciding whether a target is there before its window closes, or a classifier that must answer within a latency budget. When the cost of waiting changes on a known schedule within about one decision’s time, the look-ahead rule recovers most of what my 2025 rule gives up (a median of 98.3% on the 180 held-out schedules where my 2025 rule loses at least 0.01 points), but what it gives up is usually small. If you can solve the problem exactly, do: on this code, the exact solution takes about as long, under two seconds per schedule. To my knowledge, nobody has tried the rule in a real system.

Where it doesn’t fit

Costs that change much more slowly than a decision gain nothing: hourly prices don’t matter to a choice made in half a second. Real triage and queues don’t fit the model: their costs are random or set by policy, and their stakes are lopsided. Real driving fits only loosely, since cars fuse many sensors and plan many maneuvers, so the car at the top of the page, like the car above, is an illustration, not an application. The rule assumes two choices and a cost schedule known in advance; with evidence of unknown strength it has been checked only numerically, on a few schedules (below).

One glimpse at a time

Each peg on the machine stands for one glimpse of evidence. In a lab, the glimpses are frames of moving dots; here they are the same kind of evidence drawn as dots, in seconds this time, with the drift and noise of that study’s average observer.

Each trial, the dots drift left or right, and the evidence for “right” adds up on the strip as a noisy line. When the line reaches one of your bounds, the decision is made. High bounds are slow and accurate; low ones are fast and sloppy.

What you see is the evidence itself. In every frame, the dots stepping right minus the dots stepping left is exactly what the line adds, to the nearest dot; the bars under the line show it frame by frame. Turn on Color the steps to see which dots carry it. At 6.4% motion strength a single frame favors the right side only slightly more often than the wrong one, which is why the line moves up and down so much before it reaches a bound.

A correct choice earns 1 point and every second spent deciding costs the amount you set. Evidence grows with motion strength at 21 per second per unit of coherence, the average observer in Palmer, Huk, and Shadlen (2005); the noise has a variance of 1 per second. In a lab, part of that noise is in the observer rather than on the screen; here all of it is drawn as dots, so the screen shows everything the observer has. That’s also why the motion strength sets how fast the evidence drifts, not the share of dots on screen that move together.

The trade-off

Try a few places for the bounds and watch the expected points in the tally: they peak at one place and fall off on both sides. Too low and you pay in mistakes; too high and you pay in time. Your measured points wander around the expected ones; a few hundred decisions isn’t many.

For a cost that stays the same, the best bounds have a closed form. With evidence strength μ and the noise scaled to 1, it solves

sinh(2μb) + 2μb = μ2 / c(1)

so expensive time means low bounds, and stronger evidence means waiting for more certainty; once the evidence is strong relative to the cost, the wait also gets shorter. I’ll write B(c) for where this puts the bounds at strength 1.

When the cost of time changes

Plenty of decisions get more expensive while you make them: a reward that shrinks, a deadline, an obstacle getting closer. With a cost c(t) that changes over time, the best bounds change too. Finding it exactly takes dynamic programming: start at the end and work backward, comparing what stopping is worth with what waiting is worth, at every moment and every level of evidence.

My 2025 capstone used a simpler rule instead: at every moment, the bounds that would be best if the current cost lasted forever.

b(t) = B(c(t))(2)

My 2025 rule needs only the present cost, so it can’t see a change coming. The look-ahead rule averages the same bounds over the near future, with weights w(s) that come from the model itself:

b(t) = ∫0∞ w(s) B(c(t + s)) ds(3)

Pick a schedule, or draw your own. Everything below is computed in your browser: the best bounds by dynamic programming, both rules, and the reward each rule gives up, scored without sampling on a coarse grid. Then slide the line along the time scale. The shading ahead of it follows w(s), the stretch of the future the bounds at that moment pay attention to: it starts dark and fades as the share of the weight still to come runs out, and the tick marks where half of it has passed.

How far ahead to look

The weight w(s) says how much the cost s time units ahead should count. It comes from asking what a small rise in the cost at time t + s does to a decision sitting at a bound. It hurts only if the decision is still going then, so each future moment counts in proportion to how fast the chance of still deciding then changes as you start further from the bounds.

That has a closed form, a sum of decaying exponentials. It’s heaviest right away, with a long tail, so on average the rule looks ahead less far than a decision lasts.

Two details make it work. It averages bounds, not costs, which handles a deadline with no special case: past the deadline my 2025 rule’s bounds are at 0, and the average brings the bounds down before the deadline arrives. And the weight depends on the bounds, so the rule computes it at the bounds it produces: thirty damped passes, each just a weighted average, and no dynamic programming.

How well it holds up

I settled the rule on 90 development schedules, then fixed a test set before running anything on it: 210 new schedules from seven families (smooth changes, steps, deadlines, pulses, ramps, several steps, and piecewise levels), drawn from a seed chosen in advance.

My 2025 rule loses little. Its median loss is 0.13 points per 100 decisions, and it lost under a tenth of a point on 87 of the 210. The look-ahead rule loses less: a median of 0.0022 points, at most 0.235. On each of the 180 schedules where my 2025 rule lost at least 0.01 points, it recovered a median of 98.3% of that loss (10th percentile 90.4%, worst 55.8%).

Shifting the bounds instead doesn’t do this. Moving my 2025 rule’s bounds forward by the mean look-ahead lost more than not shifting at all on 83 of the 180 schedules without deadlines.

Exact to first order

For small changes in cost, the rule is more than a good guess. The paper proves that, to first order, the best bounds move by the weighted average of my 2025 rule’s moves, with exactly these weights; when the cost changes smoothly, the error shrinks at least as fast as the square of the change. On a 10% wiggle in the cost, c(t) = c0(1 + 0.1 sin ωt), the predicted swing is within 0.2% of the exact one and its timing within 0.24° across the paper’s test cases.

The best bounds move ahead of the cost, by close to an eighth of a cycle when the cost changes fast. My 2025 rule moves with the cost, never ahead of it. Change how fast the cost wiggles and read the gap at the line.

When the evidence’s strength is unknown

In real tasks, easy and hard trials come mixed together, so you don’t know how strong the evidence is. Then the best bounds fall over time even when the cost doesn’t: a decision that’s taking a long time is probably a hard one, and more waiting won’t help it much.

Measure the evidence in units of its own uncertainty, u = X/√(t + a), and run the clock on a log scale, τ = log((t + a)/a). Then the belief moves the same way at every moment, and a cost of c per second becomes c·(t + a) per tick: information arrives more and more slowly, so even a constant cost rises on that clock. My 2025 rule, which treats each moment as if time stood still, is the same rule on that clock. The same look-ahead rule, built with that process’s own weights, does about as well here as with known strength; the paper shows this numerically, without a proof.

What changed since 2025

In April of 2025, I presented this work as my undergraduate research capstone at the University of Cincinnati: an implementation of Jan Drugowitsch’s notes on normative solutions to the speed-accuracy trade-off. The model, its main results, and every cost sweep in the talk at σ² = 1 hold up, though a few slides misstate steps of the derivations. The code had serious errors, which I found when I re-checked it this fall. They’re fixed in the new code, with tests. The original code and talk stay online as they were.

The twelve corrections

  1. The dynamic program stopped after three iterations

    Its stopping tolerance wasn’t scaled by the time step, so it quit long before the values settled. Confidence at the bound for a cost of 0.1:

    0.5160.936

  2. Its belief grid was too coarse for its time step

    With 100 belief levels and a step of 0.0001, most of the probability never left its cell. Even fully converged, at a cost of 0.2:

    0.7420.865

  3. The belief used the hidden answer

    The belief formula included the true direction, so when the answer was left it reported the belief in the true answer rather than in “right.” Every example in the talk used right, so no number shown there changed. A case with the answer left and the evidence favoring right, belief in “right”:

    0.2030.797

  4. Its transition matrix was applied transposed

    So the belief stopped being a fair forecast of itself. Expected next belief from 0.7506:

    0.74990.7506

  5. The mean decision time dropped a factor of σ²

    Exact at σ² = 1, which every example in the talk used, and wrong elsewhere. Confidence at the best bound for σ² = 5 and a cost of 0.2:

    0.5260.620

  6. Changing costs used my 2025 rule as the answer

    It’s an approximation, the one this page measures. Two schedules both start at a cost of 0.2, and one jumps to 0.9 at t = 0.1. Their exact starting bounds differ, a confidence of 0.859 and 0.748; my 2025 rule gives 0.865 for both:

    0.8650.748for the jumping schedule

  7. Unknown strength: the drift was redrawn at every step

    In the model it’s drawn once per trial, so trials differ in difficulty. Variance of the evidence at t = 1:

    1.0041.363

  8. Unknown strength: with a biased prior, the belief didn’t match the simulation

    The starting evidence was shifted as if the prior on the drift had a nonzero mean, while the simulation still drew a zero-centered strength with the given sign. Belief in “right” at a prior of 0.8, t = 1, and X = 0:

    0.7240.8

  9. Unknown strength: a frozen-time bound at each moment

    The exact policy needs backward induction over time. Done right, the frozen-time bound starts too high. Confidence at the bound at t = 0, cost 0.2:

    0.7960.753

  10. In the talk: the bound paired with the wrong confidence

    Slide 28 pairs the bound 0.9307 with the confidence at a different point, X = 0.729, and slide 32 reuses it.

    0.811350.8655

  11. In the talk: the mean time marked as one trial’s stop

    Slides 23 and 28 treat the mean decision time as the time a single path stops. That path first crosses the bound earlier:

    0.6800.563

  12. In the talk: the σ² sweeps

    Slides 29 to 31 inherit the missing σ². Mean decision time at σ² = 10:

    0.01590.154

Smaller fixes: slide 10 calls Bayes’ rule an approximation that improves with smaller steps, when under this model it’s exact, and slides 12 and 15 put the prior inside the product over observations; the simulated beliefs on slides 16 and 32 used 1/(1 + e−X) where the slides print 1/(1 + e−2X), so one “continue” row should have stopped; np.float128, which doesn’t exist on Apple silicon or Windows; a path that never reached a bound reported as stopping at t = 0; root finders that couldn’t run; and a helper that saved a video at every time step.
